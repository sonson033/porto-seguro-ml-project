01 · What the score means

Gini 0.278 is not a percentage

Pick one person who claimed and one who didn't, at random. How often does the model put the claimer higher? A coin flip gets 50%. Perfect gets 100%. Gini slides that floor down to zero, so it reads as how far you travelled from random towards perfect.

Why not accuracy? Only 3.67% of people claim, so a model that answers “nobody ever claims” is 96.3% accurate and ranks nobody. That is literally the first row of the project's results table: 96.3% accurate, Gini 0.0000.

02 · Method

Making it a fair fight

The network is about fifteen lines of code. Nearly all the work went into making sure the comparison measured the model and nothing else.

The trap we had to design around. A network needs a validation slice to know when to stop training; a forest doesn't. Carving that slice out of the network's training data would have left it learning from 324,000 rows against the forest's 381,000 — and any gap would then be part model, part missing data. So the stopping point was fixed in advance on a separate practice run, exactly as the forest's “200 trees” was. At scoring time both models train on identical rows.

03 · Input

What both models actually see

One preprocessor, shared. If the two models saw differently-prepared data, the comparison would be measuring the preparation.

04 · Architecture

How the forest works

A decision tree asks yes/no questions until it lands in a group of at least 50 similar policies, then reports that group's historical claim rate. One tree is noisy. The forest grows 200 of them on different random slices and averages the answers.

06 · Tuning

Chosen for stability, not for the best score

Eight configurations: two architectures × two learning rates × two batch sizes. Their scores landed within 0.0036 of each other while seed-to-seed noise alone was 0.0099. Picking the highest number would have been picking noise.

09 · Cost

The surprise: the network is cheaper

We expected “slightly better but expensive.” The opposite is true. The forest walks 200 trees for every prediction; the network does two matrix multiplications.

So “neural networks are expensive” is not true here, and we can show it rather than assert it. The real costs sit somewhere else entirely.

10 · Negative results

Three things that didn't work

Worth as much as the things that did.

Why class balancing fails on a ranking metric. Weighting the rare class pushes every score in the same direction. Gini only reads the order, and shifting everything preserves the order — so in theory it changes nothing, and in practice it adds noise. We checked on logistic regression too, where it moved the score by −0.00065 against a wobble of 0.00441: indistinguishable from zero.

Shipsyes

Why the forest ships anyway. This model feeds insurance pricing. The project already flags that anonymised features make it impossible to check for hidden discrimination — a live deployment blocker. The forest at least offers tools to interrogate it; that tooling did real work earlier in the project, justifying the removal of 20 columns.

The trade on offer is: gain three correctly-ordered pairs per thousand, lose the ability to explain any individual decision. On a pricing model, that is a bad deal.

“We tested it properly instead of assuming trees were better. It is about 2% more accurate and much faster to run. We are not shipping it, because it cannot show its working — and for pricing, that matters more than 2%.”
